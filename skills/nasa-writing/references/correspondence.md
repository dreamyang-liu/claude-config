# Correspondence Mode — NAII 1450.3 Chapter 4 "Writing Standards"

## Source and status

- **Current:** NAII 1450.3, *Guidelines and Standards for NASA Letters and Memorandums*, Chapter 4 "Writing Standards", pp. 46–61. Effective 1 Nov 2024, updated 24 Jun 2025. It implements NPR 1450.10E. PDF: https://nodis3.gsfc.nasa.gov/OPD_Docs/NAII_1450_3_.pdf
- **Predecessor:** NPR 1450.10D, *NASA Correspondence Management and Communications Standards and Style*, Chapter 2 "NASA Writing Standards". Effective 24 Mar 2006, cancelled 27 Sep 2024. https://nodis3.gsfc.nasa.gov/displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_1450_010D_&page_name=Chapter2
- **Chain of authority:** NPR 1400.1I (NASA directives) says "Use NPR 1450.10 to supplement the style requirements in this directive." NPR 1450.10E (27 Sep 2024) covers correspondence process only and points to NAII 1450.3 for writing guidance.
- **Scope (NAII Introduction):** "all NASA personnel and contractors involved in the preparation, review, and distribution of written communications", for "all forms of official correspondence, including, memoranda, letters, and materials."
- **Relation to GPO:** NAII 1450.3 "supersedes the Government Publishing Office (GPO) guidance in most cases." Use the *GPO Style Manual* for detail that the NAII does not cover (Sec. 4.1).

Section numbers below are NAII 1450.3 sections. Short quotes are exact. NASA documents are US government works.

**This skill's transfer:** a chat reply, an email, a status update, or a handoff note is correspondence. The reader wants the answer, then the reason, then what to do next.

## 4.3 Organized writing

- "Open with the most important information and decrease to the least important. Avoid mere chronology."
- "Start fast, explain as necessary, then stop."
- Put the key sentence first. If that is not possible, put it before the end of the first paragraph.
- "Put requests before justifications, answers before explanations, conclusions before discussions, and summaries before details."
- For many key points, begin with a general statement of purpose.
- "Be direct." Delay the main point only to soften bad news or to introduce a controversial proposal.
- To persuade, end with a forecast, appeal, or implication. To inform, stop.

Example openers (NAII):
- "We inspected the Office of the Chief Engineer on June 24, 2024, and found its overall performance satisfactory."
- "This memorandum summarizes initial plans for reorganizing the Office of the Chief Engineer."

## 4.4 Spoken style

- Write like "people talking to people". Imagine one typical reader across the desk. Read the draft aloud.
- "Do not use contractions in formal writing" ("did not", not "didn't"). Lint: `contraction`.
- "Use the imperative mood when preparing administrative documents, especially procedures, 'how-to' instructions, and lists of duties." Example: "Sign all copies. Provide the draft by Monday."
- Before you write, answer: purpose? readers? their interests? what they know already? what will make it easy for them to understand or act?

## 4.5 Compact writing

- "Give your ideas no more words than they deserve." Shorten paragraphs to sentences, sentences to clauses, clauses to phrases, phrases to words.
- Avoid "it is" unless "it" refers to something earlier. It "tangles sentences, delays meaning, encourages passive verbs, and hides responsibility." Natural uses are acceptable: "it is time to", "it is your job to". Lint: `expletive-opener`.
  - "It is my understanding that your program covers ..." → "I understand that your program covers ..."
  - "It is recognized that as the project evolves, there likely will be changes ..." → "We recognize that as the project evolves, task definitions and priorities are likely to change." (The hedge "likely" stays.)
- Limit "there is" and "there are": "There are some deadlines that cannot be changed." → "Some deadlines cannot be changed."
- Wordy expressions (Lint: `wordy-phrase`):

| Do not say | Say |
|---|---|
| a number of | some |
| at the present time | now, at present |
| due to the fact of | because |
| for a period of | for |
| for the purpose of | for, to |
| in an effort to | to |
| in a timely manner | promptly, on time |
| in order to | to |
| in the amount of | for |
| in the near future | soon |
| on a quarterly basis | quarterly |
| the month of June | June |

- Legalistic words in nonlegal text: aforementioned → the, that, those; heretofore → until now; herewith is → here is.
- NPR 1450.10D (2.3.5, 2.3.6, 2.3.8) also said: use "-ly" words sparingly; cut doublings ("pleased OR delighted"); use short transitions (however → but, therefore → so, consequently → so, nevertheless → still). NAII 1450.3 dropped these three items.

## 4.6 Tone

- "A neutral tone is preferred."
- Take more care as the issue or the reader becomes more sensitive. "one misused word can offend the reader."
- Avoid rubber-stamp endings. They "do not improve good letters or save bad ones." For agent replies: no closing offer such as "Let me know if you have any other questions" unless a real next step exists.

## 4.7 Write positively

- "If you can accurately express an idea either positively or negatively, express it positively." Keep the negative form when you caution the reader.
  - "It will not be ready until Monday." → "It will be ready on Monday."
  - "You failed to sign the other copy." → "You need to sign the other copy."
- "Avoid several negatives in one sentence." Lint: `multiple-negatives`.
  - "A demonstration project will not be approved unless all application requirements are met." → "A demonstration project will be approved only if the applicant meets all requirements."
- Use a positive word for a negative idea: not honest → dishonest; did not remember → forgot; did not comply with → violated.
- Explain a "no" in enough detail to avoid any hint of a brush-off.

**Caution for rewriters:** a positive form must say exactly the same thing. "did not pay attention to" → "ignored" adds intent. Change it only when the source supports the stronger word.

## 4.8 Short, disciplined sentences

- "Readable sentences are simple, active, affirmative, and declarative."
- "State only one main topic in each sentence."
- "Divide long sentences into two or three short sentences. Average 20 words or less when mixing long and short sentences." Lint: `average-sentence-length` (per file), `long-sentence` (per sentence).
- Use parallel structure, especially in lists. Example of a fixed list: "a. To take minutes of all meetings. b. To answer all correspondence. c. To write monthly reports."
- "Remove all unnecessary words." Eliminate unnecessary modifiers.
- Put key ideas at the beginning or the end of the sentence. Put minor ideas in secondary clauses.
  - "It has been determined that moving the computer, as shown in Enclosure 1, would allow room for another cabinet to be installed." → "Moving the computer, as shown in Enclosure 1, would allow room for another cabinet."
- A sentence of one to six words can "drive home a key point."

## 4.9 Short paragraphs

- Each paragraph has "a single, unified topic." Short paragraphs, especially at the start.
- Put lists of items or instructions in subparagraphs or bullets, "do not use so many levels of subparagraphs that the writing becomes difficult to follow."
- A one-sentence paragraph can highlight an important idea.

## 4.10 Personal pronouns

- For the Agency, office, or group: "we", "us", "our", not "it".
- For yourself: "I", "me", "my".
- For the reader: "you".

## 4.11 Active voice

- "Use a who-does-what order." The active voice "eliminates confusion by forcing you to name the actor."
- Passive = a form of "to be" + past participle: "was received", "is being considered", "has been selected". Lint: `passive-voice`.
- Passive sentences are complete without an actor. Ask "By whom?":
  - "The material will be delivered." → "The messenger will deliver the material."
  - "The start date is to be decided." → "The contractor will decide the start date."
- "Use the passive voice only when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or obvious." Examples: "Small items are often stolen." "The applications have been mailed."

**Caution for rewriters:** each active rewrite above names an actor. Do not invent the actor. If the source does not say who acts, keep the passive or flag "actor unknown".

## 4.13 Plain language words

- "Government writing should be dignified, but it should also rely on plain language." Avoid jargon and pretentious words.

| Do not say | Say |
|---|---|
| commence | begin |
| terminate | end |
| utilize | use |
| substantial part | large part |
| because of the fact that | since, because |
| call your attention to the fact that | remind you |
| for the period of | for |
| in many instances | often |
| in the nature of | like |
| the question as to whether | whether |

- Redundant pairs — pick one word: any and all, authorize and direct, cease and desist, each and every, full and complete, order and direct, means and includes, necessary and desirable. Also "at this point in time" (→ now).
- Concrete words: write "helicopters" if you mean helicopters, not "aircraft".
- Avoid words that antagonize the reader.
- Avoid gender-specific terms: crewman → crewmember, draftsman → drafter, fireman → firefighter, foreman → supervisor, enlisted men and women → enlisted personnel. Lint: `gendered-term`.
- "who" for an individual, "that" for a group, "which" for places, objects, or animals.
- "In general, spell out numbers that are ten or less in a sentence." Use the GPO Style Manual for exceptions. (GPO uses figures for units of measurement, time, money, and percentages.) Lint: `small-number`.

## 4.14 Acronyms

- "Do not use acronyms more than necessary."
- Spell out the term at first use, with the acronym in parentheses, unless the reader knows it (FAA or NASA in a letter to the NTSB).
- "If the full title is used only once, do not identify the acronym."
- Plural acronym: lowercase s inside the parentheses: "(SUVs)".

## 4.16–4.20 Punctuation and form (from the GPO Style Manual)

- Hyphenate a unit modifier before the noun: "a drought-stricken area", "well-established criteria". No hyphen after the noun: "The criteria are well established".
- Serial comma before "and" or "or" in a series of three or more (also Ch. 1.1).
- Comma after the year in a full date inside a sentence: "the March 19, 2023, report". No comma for month and year: "the July 2024 report".
- Semicolon to separate series items that contain commas. "Do not use semicolons where commas will suffice."
- "Spell out percent unless it is part of a table, graph, or another similar representation." Lint: `percent-sign`.

## What NAII 1450.3 Ch. 4 does not cover

Units of measurement, technical notation, requirement verbs, and procedure safety blocks. Use Report, Requirement, or Procedure mode for those parts.
